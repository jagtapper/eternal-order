Onorato et al. report J1602+4228AB, nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, in The Astrophysical Journal Letters. It is the most compact known quasar-pair candidate in the Epoch of Reionization. The two quasars are 0.98 arcseconds apart, about 5.56 proper kiloparsecs (roughly 18,000 light-years) in projection, at redshift z ≈ 6.08, when the universe was about 900 million years old.

The estimated black hole masses are about 1.5 billion and 400 million suns, and both are accreting rapidly. A pair like this is about two to three orders of magnitude more likely than extrapolations of quasar clustering would suggest.

**It is still a candidate.** The authors tentatively favour a physical pair over a gravitationally lensed image, but say lensing cannot be fully ruled out yet, pending spectroscopic confirmation of a possible foreground galaxy.

## The parallel

> sahasra-yuga-paryantam ahar yad brahmaṇo viduḥ

Those who know that a day of Brahma lasts a thousand yugas, and that his night is as long, know day and night. The next verse continues: "All embodied beings emanate from the Unmanifest at the coming of the cosmic day." Bhagavad Gita 8.17–18 (Gita Press translation). Verse 8.17 also appears in the Mahabharata at 12.224.30.

## Why it sits here

Astronomers call this era cosmic dawn, when the first bright objects appeared. The Gita speaks of the manifest arising at the coming of a cosmic day, inside cycles far longer than any telescope's view. The comparison is about scale and cycle. It is not a claim that the Gita describes the Big Bang or quasars.

## The Yuga signal

**Weak this week.** Telescopes can now resolve a quasar pair from the universe's first billion years, which extends what we can see. It moves no abundance axis directly, and the pair is still a candidate.
